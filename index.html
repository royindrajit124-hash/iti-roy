Amplitude Modulation (AM) Reference Panel

Amplitude Modulation (AM) is a process in which the amplitude of a high-frequency carrier wave is varied in accordance with the instantaneous amplitude of a low-frequency message signal.

The mathematical expression for a standard full carrier AM wave (DSB-FC) is given by: $$s(t) = A_c [1 + \mu \cos(2\pi f_m t)] \cos(2\pi f_c t)$$ Where:

  • $A_c$ is the carrier amplitude.
  • $f_c$ is the carrier frequency.
  • $f_m$ is the modulating signal (message) frequency.
  • $\mu = A_m / A_c$ is the **Modulation Index** (also denoted as $m$).

Advantages:

  • Extremely simple transmitter and receiver structures (envelope detector is highly cost-efficient).
  • Narrow signal bandwidth ($2f_m$).
  • Easy sky-wave / ionospheric propagation, allowing simple receivers to pick up broadcasts over vast geographical areas.

Disadvantages:

  • Highly wasteful of power: The carrier contains no message information but carries at least 66.6% of the total transmitted power at $\mu=1$.
  • Extremely susceptible to electromagnetic noise, environmental noise, and lightning clicks which impact the wave amplitude directly.
  • AM Radio Broadcasting: In Medium Wave (MW) and Short Wave (SW) bands, facilitating intercontinental signal transmissions.
  • Air Traffic Control & Aircraft Communication: Aviation VHF communications (118–137 MHz) utilize AM instead of FM because of the *capture effect* in FM (which suppresses weaker signals). In AM, if two signals overlap, they mix, allowing controllers to detect simultaneous transmissions during emergency scenarios.
  • Single Sideband (SSB): A power-saving variant of AM used extensively in amateur radio, marine communications, and military links.
  1. Analyzing Envelope Distortion: Set Modulation Index to $\mu = 0.50$ (Under-modulation). Observe that the envelope follows the message. Change $\mu$ to $1.20$. Observe the phase reversal and notice how the diode detector chops the signal, introducing harmonic distortion.
  2. Verify Sideband Spectral Layout: Set $f_c = 300\text{ Hz}$ and $f_m = 30\text{ Hz}$. Look at the Spectrum Analyzer. Observe the three distinct spikes: the center carrier spike at $300\text{ Hz}$, the lower sideband (LSB) at $270\text{ Hz}$, and the upper sideband (USB) at $330\text{ Hz}$.
  3. RC Time Constant Clipping: Set the demodulator to **Envelope Detector**. Slide the Detector Cutoff slider to a very low frequency ($10\text{ Hz}$). Observe that the recovered wave cannot discharge fast enough, causing diagonal clipping. Slide it to $250\text{ Hz}$ and observe the high-frequency ripples of the carrier bleeding into the output.

Frequency Modulation (FM) demonstrator

Frequency Modulation (FM) varies the instantaneous frequency of the carrier signal in proportion to the message signal amplitude: $$s_{FM}(t) = A_c \cos\left(2\pi f_c t + \beta \sin(2\pi f_m t)\right)$$ where $\beta$ is the modulation index. Unlike AM, FM offers superior noise rejection (constant amplitude envelope makes it immune to amplitude spikes) but requires much wider bandwidth defined by **Carson's Rule**: $B_{FM} = 2(\Delta f + f_m)$.
